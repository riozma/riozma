"We work in BIM" is one of the most common sentences in Swiss planning offices. "We have a digital twin" is one of the most common sentences in software marketing. They are not the same thing, and the gap between them is where a lot of the next decade's work lies.

What BIM actually is

BIM (Building Information Modelling) is not a software product. It is a way of working in which the project is described by a shared model made of objects with properties: a wall knows that it is a wall, which material it is made of, its fire rating and which storey it belongs to. A 3D CAD drawing only knows lines and surfaces. That difference sounds small, but it is what makes automatic quantity take-off, clash detection between disciplines and model checking possible.

For BIM to work across companies, models need a common language. That is IFC (Industry Foundation Classes), an open ISO standard maintained by buildingSMART. In Switzerland, Bauen digital Schweiz and the SIA push this open approach, so that the engineer is not forced to use the architect's software.

Where practice is today

Most Swiss projects that "do BIM" sit at step 2 during design, and many fall back towards step 1 at handover, when the owner receives PDFs and a model nobody maintains. Real digital shadows exist mainly where monitoring was already part of the job: tunnels, dams, slopes, bridges. That is why Hexagon buying Rocscience (see the news above) is interesting: it connects step 2 geotechnical models with step 3 monitoring data, with step 4 as the stated goal. SBB's network model is another example of an owner trying to keep one model alive for decades.

Why it is hard

HurdleWhat it means in practice
IncentivesPlanners are paid for deliverables until handover. Nobody is paid to keep the model useful for operation.
Data ownershipWho owns, maintains and is liable for a model that keeps changing after handover?
InteroperabilityIFC covers design well. Sensor data, maintenance systems and simulations speak other languages.
SkillsA twin needs people who understand both the structure and the data. Few offices have them yet.

What it means for us as future planners

Two practical takeaways. First, when someone says "digital twin", ask which way the data flows. If nothing flows back from the asset, it is a model, and that is fine, but it is not a twin. Second, the most valuable work in the next years is probably not drawing better models, but designing what happens to the model after handover: which data the owner needs, which sensors are worth it, and who keeps it alive. That is a planning task, and one civil engineers are well placed to take on.
