Three stories this week share one idea. Cemvision wants to make cement from industrial residues, the Sisikon tunnel chose its explosive so the rock can be used in Lake Uri, and last week Holcim reported a third more recycled demolition material. Behind all three is the same shift: treating what used to be waste as a resource. That is the core of circular construction.

From a line to a loop

Most of the construction economy still works in a line: extract raw materials, make products, build, use, demolish, dispose. Circular construction tries to close that line into loops, so that buildings, components and materials stay in use for as long as possible and at the highest possible value. It is not the same as recycling. Recycling is one loop, and usually the outermost and least valuable one.

Why it matters in Switzerland

Construction is by far the largest material flow in the Swiss economy. Excavated and tunnelling material alone amounts to roughly 40 to 60 million tonnes per year, according to the Federal Office for the Environment (BAFU), the largest waste quantity by far. Mineral demolition waste is already widely recycled into aggregates, so the easy loop is largely closed. The real gains now lie higher up the ladder: keeping buildings, reusing components, and using binders with less clinker. At the same time, for a new, energy-efficient building the grey (embodied) emissions can be similar to, or larger than, its operating emissions over decades, which moves the focus from the boiler room to the structure.

Swiss practice already has reference projects higher up the ladder. A well-known one is the K.118 extension in Winterthur by baubüro in situ, completed in 2021 and built largely from components taken from other buildings, such as steel beams, windows and façade panels. To compare options with numbers, Swiss planners use life-cycle assessment data published by KBOB, the federal coordination body for public builders, which gives the grey emissions of common building materials. Reused components are the hard case: their emissions were already "spent" in the first building, but how to count them, and how much testing they need, is still being worked out.

InformationNobody knows exactly what is inside an existing building: which steel grade, which concrete, which connections. Reuse starts with documentation.
DesignMost buildings are glued, cast and welded together. Elements that cannot be taken apart cannot be reused.
Liability and standardsWho guarantees a reused beam? Standards are written for new products with certificates.

What it means for us as future planners

The strongest circular decisions are made early and by planners, not at the demolition site: whether to keep a building, how to join elements so they can come apart again, which binder to specify, and where excavated material will go, as the Sisikon tunnel shows. These choices need engineers who can assess existing structures, document materials and accept a little more uncertainty in exchange for much less waste.
